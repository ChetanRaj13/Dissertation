Executive Summary / Key Findings
A comprehensive inventory of Virginia's data center market reveals that the Commonwealth hosts over 300 operational facilities totaling dozens of millions of square feet and multi-gigawatt contracted IT loads. With Loudoun County approaching severe power saturation, major new development has expanded rapidly into Prince William County (including the controversial Digital Gateway), Culpeper, Fauquier, and Central Virginia. The audit details power requirements, substation interconnections, and the growing tension between rapid load growth and community resistance.

Utility Interconnections, Substations, and Future Pipeline

Dominionâs numbers set the terms of every Virginia debate. Its contracted data center pipeline was 53.8 GW in July 2026, up from 16.5 GW three years earlier, and the State Corporation Commissionâs November 2025 rate order created a new GS-5 class for customers of 25 megawatts or more: 14-year contracts, minimum charges of 85 percent of contracted transmission and distribution demand and 60 percent of generation demand, collateral for weaker credits, and exit fees. In July 2026 the SCC went further and ordered Dominion to bill large customers directly for the substations and line connections they require. Capacity prices tell the other half of the story: the Dominion zone cleared PJMâs last three auctions at or near the price cap, about $330 per megawatt-day, and PJMâs emergency backstop auction for roughly 6 GW of new supply opens September 30, with data centers energizing after June 2027 told to bring their own power or face curtailment. Appalachian Power, meanwhile, is negotiating with 36 large customers totaling 25,000 MW, more than triple its own peak, and NOVEC says data centers will be 95 percent of its sales by 2032.

The General Assemblyâs December 2024 JLARC study found data centers paying their full cost of service today but warned that unconstrained growth could add $14 to $37 a month to a typical residential bill by 2040. The 2026 session passed fifteen data center bills, including utility recovery of generation costs from 25 MW+ customers and monthly water reporting, then deadlocked over the sales tax exemption, which cost $1.9 billion in fiscal 2025. The June 22 budget compromise kept the exemption through 2035 and imposed a new Data Center Electricity Consumption Tax of 1.1 cents per kilowatt-hour, capped at $600 million a year and effective July 1, 2026. Governor Spanberger declined a statewide moratorium on August 13, then on September 18 signed Executive Order 22 and a Data Center Accountability Framework: no more nondisclosure agreements on projects, an end to by-right approval above 25 MW, removal from fast-track permitting, water and noise standards, and limits on on-site gas generation, with the legislative pieces due in the 2027 session.

The localities moved first.
